## Foreword

2025 reinforced what many GRC leaders have been feeling for the past several years: the work is expanding, the expectations are accelerating, and the margin for error is shrinking. Risk and compliance teams are being asked to demonstrate readiness more frequently, respond faster when something goes wrong, and provide clearer proof of control effectiveness to stakeholders across the business.

At the same time, third-party ecosystems continue to grow, introducing new operational dependencies and new categories of risk that cannot be managed through point-in-time reviews alone. As AI becomes embedded across workflows and vendor offerings, GRC teams are increasingly expected to govern not only traditional security and compliance risks, but the rapidly evolving risks associated with AI-enabled technology.

The findings of this year’s survey point to a market investing in maturity but still working through the operational realities of scaling. Many organizations report strong confidence in their ability to execute: most respondents said they had the budget and resources needed to achieve their goals in 2025, and a majority anticipate spending more money on GRC in 2026. At the same time, the underlying workload pressure is clear. Organizations expect to spend more time on IT risk management and compliance in the year ahead, and breach experience appears to be a meaningful accelerant: respondents who experienced a breach are significantly more likely to anticipate increased time investment in 2026 than those who did not. Taken together, this suggests that even when resourcing is available, the central challenge becomes how to scale readiness without scaling administrative overhead in parallel.

So, what does this mean for 2026?

The short answer? GRC operating models and tooling choices matter. Respondents who manage risk ad-hoc are far more likely to have experienced a breach than those using an integrated, automated approach. Many organizations report high maturity and widespread adoption of third-party risk management best practices, yet their execution inputs remain mixed, with continued reliance on spreadsheets and document-based questionnaires. This gap reflects an important reality for 2026: maturity is no longer defined by intent or policy alone. It is defined by consistency, traceability, and the ability to maintain assurance over time across controls, evidence, and third parties.

As you explore the benchmarks and insights throughout this report, we invite you to consider where your program sits on the maturity curve and what it will take to move forward this year. The collective experience of your peers points to a clear direction: teams are prioritizing centralization, standardization, and automation to reduce manual effort and sustain continuous readiness. Whether you are formalizing an emerging program or evolving a mature one, the opportunity in 2026 is to turn investment into measurable outcomes that result in less time spent chasing evidence, clearer reporting for leadership, and stronger resilience when risks materialize.

## 01 How GRC Leaders Are Scaling and Operating Their Compliance Programs

Compliance teams are being asked to do more than ever: keep pace with expanding requirements, demonstrate control effectiveness faster, and deliver audit-ready evidence with fewer bottlenecks. Most organizations are organizing GRC work in ways that look mature on paper, like centralized teams, regular audits, and increasing use of controls monitoring.

However, the day-to-day reality still includes heavy manual overhead, fragmented systems, and cross-functional chasing that slows everything down. The biggest differentiator in this chapter isn’t simply whether a program is centralized; it’s whether the work is executed through integrated, repeatable workflows that keep controls and evidence current year-round.

This year’s data shows a clear picture of where this friction still persists, especially in manual work, cross-team coordination, and evidence readiness. It also underscores a consistent theme across the benchmark: the structure of your compliance operations — where it’s centralized, distributed across your organization, automated, or addressed ad-hoc — is closely tied to compliance outcomes. This points to a consistent operational reality: organizations are structurally centralizing GRC and increasingly adopting monitoring software, but they still lose significant time to manual work and fragmented processes.

The strongest differentiator across our findings is not just whether a team is centralized. It’s whether risk and compliance workflows are executed in an integrated, automated way that supports continuous evidence readiness and control assurance.

> Organizations are structurally centralizing GRC and increasingly adopting monitoring software, but they still lose significant time to manual work and fragmented processes.

### How compliance is structured and governed

Most organizations report having centralized ownership for governance, risk, and compliance. **86%** of respondents have a centralized team responsible for GRC efforts, while only **14%** said that individual teams or business units handle GRC. Centralization has become the dominant operating model, but centralization alone doesn’t guarantee that risk and compliance are truly integrated in practice.

- **86%**: We have a centralized team responsible for our GRC efforts
- **14%**: Individual teams or business units handle their own GRC efforts

Many organizations still treat compliance and risk as separate activities rather than a unified system. When asked how risk and compliance functions are structured, **57%** reported that risk and compliance are integrated functions, while **36%** said they are separate functions, with each having its own staff. **7%** said it varies by function or location. Only about one in five respondents has an integrated view where risk and compliance are explicitly tied together. The more common reality is that compliance helps reduce risk, but the two functions still tend to activate in response to different triggers, leading to duplicated efforts, unclear ownership, and gaps in visibility.

Notably, program structure influences whether organizations experience consistency or variability: respondents with individual teams and/or business units were more likely to say risk/compliance structure varies by business function and/or location (**11%**) than those with a centralized team (**6%**). This aligns with a practical reality: distributed models may better reflect how work gets done locally, but they often introduce inconsistencies in how controls are implemented, evaluated, and evidenced.

When asked about the purpose of compliance, only **21%** of those who have a centralized team responsible for GRC reported having an integrated view of how to manage their risks. In comparison, **43%** reported that risk and compliance activities are conducted in response to separate events. Another **36%** said that their organization simply views compliance as the function that enforces regulatory standards.

> **The takeaway?** Distributed programs are more likely to interpret compliance as a reactive function, an outcome that can add friction when organizations need fast, proactive reporting on control posture and risk exposure.

### What’s slowing teams down during day-to-day compliance work

Even where compliance programs appear structurally mature, the operational reality is that many teams are still wrestling with manual, repetitive work and fragmented processes. Respondents most often cited challenges that reflect a common root issue: lack of centralized visibility and workflow continuity.

When asked about their most time-consuming compliance tasks, the top reported issues were switching between multiple systems, identifying where critical risks are, and finding risk-related information. These are the hallmarks of a workflow problem, not a talent problem. When compliance teams are forced to operate across multiple sources of truth, every audit, control test, and report becomes a mini data-integration project.

The tasks that closely follow when effort turns into coordination: following up with staff in other departments, ensuring consistency in how controls are applied across an organization, and maintaining policies and tracing evidence of control operation. These tasks are often less about technical complexity and more about operational fragmentation, which are precisely the conditions that make compliance difficult to scale.

### The audit prep burden is growing

Preparing for and executing audits is also time-consuming. Audit execution tasks point to a consistent operational pressure: access reviews, evidence retrieval, and auditor back-and-forth dominate where teams spend time. These are not edge-case problems. They’re foundational compliance operations that repeat every cycle and scale poorly as the business grows.

The largest time sinks align closely with evidence workflows and access governance. These tasks are foundational — not fringe — and they represent a major opportunity for organizations to reduce audit cycle time by streamlining how evidence is collected, validated, stored, and permissioned. If teams want to reduce audit cycle time and reduce audit fatigue, the path is not “work harder” or “hire more.” It’s to automate evidence collection, standardize ownership and approvals, and reduce one-off follow-ups by keeping evidence continuously up to date.

### Administrative overhead remains a material constraint

Manual work remains one of the most consistent signals in this year’s data, and one of the clearest levers for improving compliance efficiency. A major share of respondents report that a meaningful chunk of risk/compliance time is spent on repetitive administrative work, with the largest concentration in the 30%–50% range.

Even more telling: Organizations that operate ad-hoc or in silos show a higher average admin-time burden than those using integrated, mostly automated approaches. **76%** of respondents report spending 30% or more of their time on repetitive tasks, which hasn’t changed much year over year (2025’s results came in at 77%).

Administrative overhead doesn’t just slow down compliance tasks. It reduces the time available for higher-value work, like proactive risk analysis, control design improvements, and readiness planning. It also creates a compounding effect: the more time is spent on administration, the harder it becomes to shift toward continuous monitoring and continuous evidence readiness.

Compliance capacity is currently being spent on staying afloat rather than improving controls, strengthening risk decisions, or preparing for future requirements. This is where automation creates compounding returns, because time saved here flows into every other part of the program.

However, organizations using an integrated, automated tool have a lower average administrative time burden ($\approx 33\%$) than those managing IT risk ad-hoc (39%) or in siloed departments (39%). This relationship is directionally consistent: integrated workflows reduce the coordination and reconciliation work that drives administrative load.

### Continuous compliance is advancing (unevenly)

The data suggests that many organizations are taking steps toward more continuous compliance behaviors, but respondents still have a meaningful gap between intent and practice. When asked about their approach to evidence collection, respondents most commonly said they regularly collect evidence for internal and external audits (**54%**), while **25%** collect on an ongoing basis as part of a continuous compliance program, **11%** use an ad-hoc basis, and **10%** collect only for external audits.

> **The takeaway?** Ongoing evidence collection is substantial, but it is not yet the dominant practice.

Control evaluation patterns show a similar split between broad assurance and risk-based prioritization. **49%** of respondents evaluate the effectiveness of all controls, and **41%** evaluate only the most critical controls, while **9%** evaluate controls solely for the purpose of meeting upcoming audit requirements. Routine control evaluation is becoming standard, but organizations vary widely on whether this evaluation is comprehensive or targeted.

The move toward continuous compliance is apparent when looking at controls monitoring as well. We asked respondents about their approach to controls monitoring, and **58%** reported that they use software to continuously monitor controls, while others conduct manual reviews or rely on external assessors.

### Outsourcing as a strategy for scalability

Outsourcing remains a meaningful part of how organizations scale compliance and security programs, particularly for specialized tasks and high-effort operational needs. Outsourcing is widely used, particularly for specialized and execution-heavy work such as security testing and risk assessments. Many organizations also rely on external support for policy generation and security/asset management. At the same time, fewer outsource the core operational backbone of compliance, such as compliance management or defining a unified control framework.

Company size patterns are instructive. Smaller organizations are more likely to outsource risk assessments and security/asset management than the largest organizations. This likely reflects practical constraints: limited internal bandwidth, limited specialization, and a need to accelerate execution. Larger organizations may bring more capabilities in-house, but their complexity increases demands for standardization, coordination, and visibility.

### Using AI to streamline workflows

AI adoption in GRC has moved decisively beyond experimentation and into day-to-day execution. Only **3%** of respondents say they are not using AI to streamline any workflows, demonstrating how quickly AI is becoming a baseline productivity layer for many teams rather than a niche capability used by early adopters.

What stands out is where AI is being applied. The highest-use workflows are documentation-heavy activities that historically consume substantial time and slow down delivery, like reviewing documentation (**52%**) and merging multiple documents. These are the exact areas where GRC teams often face bottlenecks, because they require synthesizing information across sources, mapping it to standards, and producing consistent outputs.

AI is also being used as a research and analysis accelerator, not just a writing assistant. More than half of respondents (**52%**) use AI for research, indicating that teams are increasingly relying on AI to gather context, summarize requirements, and support decision-making quickly. At the same time, a significant share are using AI for writing policies (**41%**).

One of the most revealing findings is what remains comparatively low: responding to security questionnaires (**27%**). Given how frequently questionnaires are cited as a pain point in many organizations, this suggests that while teams see value in using AI for core internal workflows, fewer have fully operationalized AI for external-facing assurance processes.

### Outcomes: The connection between breaches and approaches to risk management

The compliance operating model is not just an efficiency question. It’s closely tied to risk outcomes. Respondents who manage IT risk ad-hoc report markedly higher breach rates than respondents using an integrated and mostly automated approach. **50%** of those managing risk ad-hoc experienced a breach in 2025, vs. only **27%** using an integrated, automated tool. This mirrors our survey results for the last three years, emphasizing that organizations with integrated, automated approaches are better positioned to reduce risks and shorten the time from signal to action.

Breach cost distribution adds weight to the operational argument. A meaningful share of respondents report breach costs in the multi-million-dollar range, with **1 in 4 respondents (25%)** reporting costs of **$5M or more**.

Among respondents who experienced a security breach in the last 24 months, **58%** anticipate spending more time on IT risk management and compliance in 2026 (43% slightly more time and 15% significantly more time). Organizations that have experienced a breach are much less likely to expect time to stay flat (21% vs 37%), meaning that after an incident, teams anticipate additional work that goes beyond immediate response.

## 02 Framework Adoption Trends and Risk Management Best Practices

Risk management programs are evolving in two directions at once. Organizations are adopting a broader set of frameworks, conducting assessments on a more regular cadence, and formalizing governance practices that support repeatability. At the same time, the data shows a persistent execution gap, and program maturity is uneven. 

This chapter focuses on what that maturity looks like in practice: which standards organizations are using, how they handle regional variance, how frequently they reassess risk, and which operating models are most strongly associated with consistent execution. Together, these results point to a clear conclusion: as scope expands, the programs that perform best are those that can keep risk and compliance execution repeatable, measurable, and continuously up-to-date.

### The compliance framework footprint is broad (and AI governance is now mainstream)

Respondents report broad framework adoption, with over half of respondents (**53%**) using **ISO 27001** and **48%** using **NIST CSF**. ISO 27001 remains a leading framework, and SOC frameworks remain widely used for assurance-driven programs. Privacy regulation alignment is also substantial, with meaningful representation across GDPR and U.S. state privacy requirements.

The most notable signal this year is the significant shift in AI governance from early adoption to mainstream planning. **ISO 42001** and the **NIST AI Risk Management Framework** appear at levels that suggest many organizations are actively operationalizing AI risk, not merely discussing it. This has two practical implications: first, risk programs are now covering broader scope than traditional security and privacy; second, organizations will increasingly benefit from being able to map overlapping requirements across frameworks rather than managing them separately.

tends to fall into one of three approaches. The most common approach (56%) is rationalization

compliance into a series of one-off interpretations and exceptions.
The most scalable approaches are the ones that reduce complexity at the point
of execution: standardizing controls, harmonizing evidence, and ensuring reporting is
consistent regardless of geography.

CHAPTER 3
Budgets, Capacity, and ROI: How GRC
Teams Are Funding the Work in 2026
Budgets and resourcing decisions reveal how organizations translate GRC priorities into operational reality. This year’s
benchmark shows that GRC is not being treated as a discretionary function. Many organizations are sustaining or increasing
investment, and reported 2026 budget levels indicate that GRC programs are expected to deliver measurable value across
multiple fronts: regulatory readiness, customer assurance, risk reduction, and resilience.
However, this is also where an important pattern emerges across the last several years. Survey respondents consistently
report that budgets are increasing year over year, while many GRC leaders hear a different story in the market: tightened
spending, delayed purchases, and scrutiny on renewals.

53 // 2026 IT Risk and Compliance Benchmark Report hyperproof.io
The most likely explanation is not that one view is “wrong,” but that they are measuring different realities. This survey
captures the outlook and expectations of GRC leaders at a point in time, often reflecting planned budgets, priority-setting,
or early-year allocations. Market sentiment, by contrast, often reflects later-stage adjustments such as mid-year freezes,
reforecasting, shifting spend to fewer vendors, or reallocations that reduce discretionary projects while preserving
mission-critical security and compliance work.
In other words, organizations may still feel pressure to invest in GRC, even as they face constraints on how that investment
shows up in headcount, tooling, and external services.
This chapter examines the budget story through three lenses. First, it establishes baseline context through revenue and
team size, because program complexity increases as organizations scale. Second, it looks at budget direction, the drivers
behind budget changes, and what organizations are planning for 2026. Third, it evaluates how funding is being allocated
across headcount, software, and external services, and what that allocation implies for ROI. Across these findings,
one conclusion stands out: in an environment where planned investment and actual spend can diverge, the strongest
return comes from building an operating model that holds up under change, supported by standardized processes and
technology that reduces manual overhead and strengthens traceability.

CHAPTER 4
Third-Party Risk Management at Scale
Third-party risk management (TPRM) has moved from a specialized security practice to a business-wide operating
requirement. Vendors and suppliers are now embedded in nearly every critical workflow, from customer data processing and
core infrastructure to finance, HR, and AI-enabled services. As a result, third-party risk is no longer something organizations
can address only at onboarding or at annual renewal. It requires repeatable decision-making, consistent evidence
collection, and the ability to detect changes in vendor risk posture over time.
This year’s data reflects that shift in two important ways. First, third-party risk programs are increasingly expected to
deliver ongoing assurance, not just point-in-time due diligence. Respondents’ priorities emphasize cybersecurity and data
protection, and many organizations report maturity models that include continuous monitoring.
Second, the operational inputs powering TPRM remain varied. Organizations continue to rely on a mix of questionnaires,
trust centers, and risk rating solutions, along with manual tools and fragmented workflows. In practice, these mixed inputs
often create the biggest execution challenges: normalizing information, applying consistent evaluation standards, tracking
remediation, and maintaining an audit-ready record as vendors and requirements change.

81 // 2026 IT Risk and Compliance Benchmark Report hyperproof.io
This chapter also highlights how the scope of third-party risk is expanding in step with technology adoption. AI is
now part of the vendor ecosystem, and many organizations have begun incorporating AI-specific assessment
criteria into supplier evaluations. However, approaches differ substantially, ranging from dedicated frameworks
to lighter-weight criteria within general assessments. This variability signals an important maturity frontier:
organizations recognize AI-related third-party risk, but many are still formalizing what “good” looks like in practice,
including what evidence to request, how to validate it, and how frequently to reassess.
Across these findings, the underlying theme is consistency at scale. As vendor ecosystems grow, the primary
constraint is not intent. It is the ability to run third-party risk management as a standardized, repeatable workflow
that integrates evidence, monitoring, and remediation. Programs that can centralize vendor risk records, apply
consistent controls and evaluation standards, and reduce manual follow-up are best positioned to keep pace with
rising assurance expectations without turning TPRM into a bottleneck for the business.

82 // 2026 IT Risk and Compliance Benchmark Report hyperproof.io
Tooling is improving, but many programs still
rely on manual inputs
Even as third-party risk programs mature, the tools and inputs teams rely on remain mixed. Many organizations
are combining dedicated solutions and structured processes with manual workflows that slow down reviews and
introduce inconsistency.
A key implication is that third-party risk often becomes “multi-system by default.” When vendor evidence,
questionnaires, ratings, and remediation tracking are spread across different tools, teams spend more time
normalizing information than making informed decisions. This can create friction in vendor onboarding, delay
remediation follow-through, and make it harder to demonstrate consistency to auditors and leadership. The
organizations best positioned to scale are those that can centralize third-party risk records, standardize evidence
requirements, and automate repeatable steps, such as collection, parsing, and status tracking.

CHAPTER 5
Decision Power in GRC: Ownership,
Oversight, and Organizational Readiness
GRC decision-making has become more consequential — and more visible — than it was even a few years ago. Risk and
compliance leaders are no longer making choices solely to satisfy auditors or check regulatory boxes. They are making
decisions that directly affect how quickly the business can onboard vendors, enter new markets, meet customer assurance
demands, and respond when something goes wrong. In this environment, the structure of decision-making, who holds
oversight, and how teams are staffed are not internal details. They shape how effectively an organization can maintain
readiness and prove it under scrutiny.

107 // 2026 IT Risk and Compliance Benchmark Report hyperproof.io
This year’s data suggests a clear tension at the center of many programs. Decision authority is often concentrated in a
small set of leaders, which can enable speed and accountability. At the same time, GRC execution increasingly depends on
distributed participation across IT, security, legal, procurement, and business owners who must provide evidence, perform
control activities, and respond to issues on tight timelines. When governance and operating structure are clear, this distribution
becomes manageable through standardized workflows and consistent evidence practices. When governance is unclear,
distribution becomes fragmentation, leading to inconsistent control application, heavier follow-up burden, and slower
reporting.
Organizations are planning for continued growth in compliance capacity, reflecting the expectation that risk and compliance
work will require sustained effort in 2026 and beyond. However, growth introduces its own operational challenges. Larger
teams and broader programs require stronger process discipline, clearer ownership, and systems that make day-to-day work
repeatable. Without that structure, scaling can increase coordination cost rather than increasing throughput and consistency.
GRC technology decisions are rarely made by one person in isolation. Even where one role holds primary responsibility, buying
decisions typically reflect a committee dynamic with distinct champions, influencers, and financial approvers. The most
effective organizations treat this as an advantage: they align stakeholders around a shared definition of success, connect
investments to measurable outcomes, and choose operating models and tools that support standardization at scale.

The buying committee is clear, with distinct
champions, influencers, and financial approvers
When respondents describe who is involved in buying compliance or risk technology, a consistent pattern emerges:
organizations tend to have clear champions, clear influencers, and clear financial approvers. This is important because it
highlights that buying decisions are rarely one-dimensional. Even when a single leader owns the decision, the purchase is
typically shaped by operational users, security leadership, and finance.
For practitioners, the key implication is that successful technology decisions require alignment on both outcomes and
adoption. Champions often focus on solving operational pain, influencers often evaluate fit and risk reduction, and financial
approvers assess ROI and consolidation opportunities. The strongest buying cases connect these priorities. They tie day-to-
day execution improvements, such as reduced manual work and faster evidence collection, to defensible outcomes leadership
cares about: readiness, consistency, and reduced exposure.
Organizations that treat these as separate conversations often struggle with adoption later. Programs that align on a shared
operating model and common requirements before purchase are better positioned to implement efficiently and demonstrate
measurable gains after the platform is in place.

Oversight sits high, but the “highest level owner”
varies by organization profile
Oversight of compliance and risk management often sits at senior levels, which reflects the strategic importance
of trust and defensibility. Senior oversight can help prioritize investments, break cross-functional bottlenecks,
and ensure that compliance and risk work receive the organizational attention they require. It also signals that

At the same time, where oversight is placed influences how programs operate. When oversight is clearly assigned
and connected to a centralized operating model, programs are more likely to set standard expectations for
controls, evidence, and reporting. Where oversight is less defined or varies across business units, it is more
common to see fragmented processes and inconsistent documentation standards, which creates variability in
readiness and greater manual effort for teams trying to consolidate information for leadership.
