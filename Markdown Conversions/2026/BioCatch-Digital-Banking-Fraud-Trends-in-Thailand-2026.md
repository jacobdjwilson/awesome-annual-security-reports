Thai banks in 2026 grapple with protecting their customers from investment fraud
rings and call center scams, while working to unveil sprawling networks of mule
accounts across the entire Thai banking system. Criminal groups pose as bank
officials, police officers, or representatives of government agencies, persuading
victims to transfer funds and install malicious remote-access applications. The
rapid growth of mobile banking and real-time transfers via PromptPay has
accelerated digital adoption across Thailand, but it’s also shrunk the window in
which banks have an opportunity to detect and stop authorized push payment
(APP) scams. Readily available AI tools, social media targeting, and cross-border
scam compounds operating in the region have industrialized social engineering
and expanded its reach.

Thai banks are also navigating a shifting regulatory and policy environment. The
Bank of Thailand has introduced stricter measures to combat mule accounts,
strengthen digital identity verification, and improve transaction monitoring, while
collaborating with the Thai Bankers’ Association and telecom providers to disrupt
scam networks more quickly. Expectations around customer protection and
reimbursement are rising, alongside requirements to balance frictionless digital
experiences with stronger authentication and real-time fraud controls.

This survey offers a snapshot of how fraud and financial-crime leaders at Thai
banks see the moment: where operational strain is intensifying, which threats pose
the greatest systemic risk, and how prepared institutions feel for the technological,
criminal, and regulatory shifts reshaping Thailand’s financial ecosystem.

ABOUT THIS SURVEY

BioCatch commissioned a series of anonymous surveys of a combined
1,200 banking leaders in 12 different geographies. All respondents
were of manager level or above and worked in their bank’s fraud or
financial crime department. One hundred individuals were surveyed
in each of the Benelux region (Belgium, the Netherlands, and
Luxembourg), France, Germany, Indonesia, Italy, the Nordics (Denmark,
Finland, Norway, and Sweden), Saudi Arabia, Singapore, South Africa,
Switzerland, Thailand, and the United Arab Emirates.

The surveys were conducted between Dec. 15, 2025 and Jan. 6, 2026.

Within the respondents from Thailand, 75% identified themselves
as the primary decision maker within their departments, while 21%
said they influenced decision-making. 34% of those surveyed were
managers or senior managers, while 23% identified as directors or
senior directors. The remaining 43% were vice president level or
above. 18% were members of their bank's C-suite.

The majority of those surveyed in Thailand report fraud attempts increasing at their institution — in line with both regional and
global averages. We see concern intensify among senior leaders: Nearly three-quarters of Thai banking leaders of vice president
level or above reported increases in fraud activity at their organization in the last year. Thai respondents identified social
engineering scams and mule accounts as their top fraud concerns, highlighting the growing impact of organized crime and the
money laundering networks through which it launders its money.

The same pattern holds for losses: While a little more than half of those surveyed in Thailand saw fraud losses rising at their bank,
the C-suite once again painted a far grimmer picture, with 78% of respondents reporting growing fraud losses. The gap suggests
executives may recognize the aggregate financial and reputational impact of fraud, particularly as large-scale scam operations
and organized fraud networks continue to target Southeast Asian markets.

What stands out most is the scale of Thailand’s financial exposure. Eight in 10 Thai banking leaders surveyed estimate their bank’s
annual fraud losses at greater than $5 million — well above neighboring countries and even exceeding the global average. Among
the C-suite, this was unanimous, with all C-level execs reporting annual losses of greater than $5 million, 55% reporting annual
fraud losses of greater than $10 million, and 16% indicating their bank lost more than $25 million to fraud every year.

A survey of banking leaders on fraud risk in Thailand02 Fraud controls and efficiency

While, overall, Thai banking leaders expressed confidence in their
existing fraud defenses, a closer examination reveals signs of
operational strain. 39% do not rate their fraud controls as effective,
placing Thailand behind both neighboring markets and global
benchmarks on perceived control strength.

Nearly one-third of those surveyed in Thailand reported false positive
rates of greater than 60%, while 71% of respondents said their existing
fraud solutions came with a false positive rate above 40%. Both figures
are greater than regional and global averages.

Only 24% of those surveyed said their bank fully investigates fraud cases
within one day. Machine learning adoption among respondent banks
stands at 42%, again trailing both neighboring markets and the global
average. This combination of elevated false positives, slower case
resolution, and comparatively lower advanced model usage suggests
existing controls are not yet delivering optimal precision or speed.

Thai banks do appear to recognize the need for improvement. While
just 10% of those surveyed indicated their organization was actively
upgrading its fraud defenses, 85% said their bank was at least
exploring new solutions.

consequences of fraud

Thai banking leaders indicate the
reputational damage inflicted by fraud
and scams concerns them more than
any economic consequences. Fraud in
Thailand appears not only a financial
vulnerability but also a trust and brand
risk that can quickly escalate, particularly
in a highly connected and socially active
market where customer complaints and
scam stories can spread rapidly.

Interestingly, those working on fraud
teams at Thai banks report less concern
about reputational damage (50%),
suggesting their focus may be more
operational and loss-driven. This split
points to a strategic tension. Operational
teams manage immediate fraud impact,
while leadership weighs direct financial
exposure, potentially underestimating
how closely financial loss and reputational
harm are intertwined in the long term.

With 48% of Thai respondents indicating their bank
reimburses at least half of scam victims, Thailand's
reimbursement rate both aligns with those of
neighboring markets and sits above the global
average. The largest share falls in the 51–75%
reimbursement range, indicating many organizations
are willing to absorb a substantial portion of losses to
preserve customer relationships and trust.

At the same time, relatively few Thai banking leaders

organization, while a notable portion capped reimbursement at
50% or below. This suggests a more balanced stance, protecting
customers while maintaining shared responsibility. The distribution
reflects an industry navigating the trade-off between customer
expectations, financial impact, and the risk of creating a moral
hazard, especially as scam volumes continue to rise.

Human behavior

Thai respondents reported a higher adoption rate
of behavioral solutions than banking leaders in any
other of the 12 geographies surveyed.

While 44% of Thai banking leaders
indicated current use of a behavior-based solution,
an additional 32% planned to incorporate one,
showing continued momentum. More than half
of those surveyed in Thailand view behavioral
solutions as highly valuable. This figure rises to
70% among those working on fraud teams in the
country.

Thai banks clearly recognize transaction data
alone is no longer enough to combat the fraud
(social engineering, mule recruitment, deepfakes,
AI tools, etc.) of the present or the future.

A survey of banking leaders on fraud risk in Thailand05  The future of fraud in Thailand:

Mule network challenges

Thai banking leaders overwhelmingly (97%) report
feeling prepared for Bank of Thailand
expectations around authentication, mule
prevention, and PromptPay security, indicating
strong regulatory alignment and internal focus on
tightening controls.

At the same time, nearly all respondents (87%)
say that identifying mule accounts reused
across multiple institutions remains a significant
operational hurdle. This highlights the structural
nature of mule activity, how accounts are not
confined to a single bank, and detection becomes
far more complex once funds and identities move
across institutional boundaries.

While individual Thai banks may feel prepared
from a policy and control standpoint, effective
mule disruption will increasingly depend on
cross-bank intelligence sharing, ecosystem-
level monitoring, and faster collective response
mechanisms rather than siloed defenses.

Real-time risk

Real-time transfers are widely viewed as a
significant fraud risk in Thailand, with 92% of Thai
banking leaders recognizing in instant payments a
very high or moderate risk for fraud. The speed of
instant payments compresses decision windows
and limits recovery options, making them the
preferred channel for moving funds through mule
accounts once a victim has been compromised.

This directly connects to the mule challenge
highlighted on the previous page. When mule
accounts are reused across multiple banks,
instant transfers allow fraud networks to fragment
and disperse funds within seconds, reducing
traceability. In that context, managing real-time
risk is not only about stronger authentication at
the point of payment, but also about coordinated,
cross-bank visibility to detect and disrupt mule
activity before funds disperse throughout
the system.
