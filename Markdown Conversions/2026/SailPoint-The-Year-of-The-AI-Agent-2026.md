## The Challenge of Identity Security

The challenge of identity security is rapidly growing in both scale and complexity.

Organizations must provide safe and efficient access to an ever-changing tapestry of global employees and contractors. Now, they must also secure a torrent of non-human identities—most notably, autonomous AI agents—working behind the scenes around the clock. Securing access for these identities at scale without slowing down business operations is a massive challenge.

While inefficient human onboarding processes and standing permissions have always opened doors to risk, governing these non-human actors introduces unprecedented challenges. The uncertainty in the market stems from the fact that this is entirely new territory. To truly grasp the scope of the problem, we must define AI agents into their rightful categories, as enterprise risk fundamentally correlates with an agent’s level of autonomy and how it is deployed. For example:

- **Assisted & packaged agents (Lower risk)**: These act as everyday copilots. They often function as “Client” agents, leveraging existing enterprise credentials to access internal systems. Because a human remains strictly in the loop to drive execution, the security footprint is relatively contained.
- **Low-code & custom-coded agents (Variable to high risk)**: These empower businesses to automate complex workflows, but the risk profile dramatically spikes depending on their data access and whether they hold broad, standing permissions. This risk is heavily compounded by the rise of “Shadow AI”—where employees quietly bypass IT protocols to utilize external LLM tools or download unauthorized local agents directly to their machines, creating massive visibility gaps.
- **Fully autonomous & multi-agent systems (Highest risk)**: Representing the true “agentic workforce,” these sophisticated entities operate independently in the cloud or across third-party partner ecosystems. With minimal-to-no human oversight, these interconnected agents can self-plan, collaborate, and adapt. They introduce the most profound organizational risks, including cascading machine-speed security breaches, untraceable compliance violations, and unpredictable emergent behaviors.

Managing this diverse spectrum of AI agents requires novel approaches, and frankly, many organizations are unaware of the underlying security issues because there are very few good examples being set. As a result, the traditional access models that once worked for human-only environments are now obsolete and may not keep up with the vast mix of human and machine identities moving across today’s enterprise environments.

To understand how organizations are preparing to secure this new landscape in 2026—a year rapidly defining itself as the year of the AI agent—we surveyed 100 security and identity leaders from Fortune 500 and Global 2000 companies.

The adoption of AI agents is moving quickly, but if you’re still figuring out how to securely govern this autonomous workforce, you have not fallen too far behind just yet. While the vast majority of organizations are rushing to deploy agents, our research shows that governance policies are still in development at 57% of those same companies. However, the market is sitting at the precipice of a major inflection point. As these non-human identities multiply and gain access to highly sensitive data, now is the time to get serious about this shift before the market leaves you behind. Any organization utilizing AI without proper governance is already vulnerable; as the volume of these agents grows, the probability that a machine-speed breach or compliance failure actually happens grows higher and higher.

Let’s walk through some of the most pressing agent-related security challenges and market takeaways that can help your organization as it figures out how to approach this new frontier. We’ll explore the current realities of AI agent deployment, the dangerous visibility gaps preventing secure innovation, and how an identity-centric framework—underpinned by robust data access security—provides a secure, compliant path forward for both human and non-human identities.

## Executive Summary

A fundamental shift is taking root across the enterprise as organizations move beyond conversational AI and begin actively deploying a new breed of action-oriented AI agents. While still in its early stages, this transition introduces unprecedented security challenges, identity blind spots, and a complex web of governance and compliance demands that many organizations are unprepared to meet.

![Infographic highlighting 91% of organizations deploying or evaluating AI agents]

With **91%** of organizations already deploying or evaluating AI agents, this is no longer a future concern—it is an immediate reality. This urgency is driven by two colliding forces: first, employees are rapidly creating and using unsanctioned “shadow AI” agents on their own; and second, enterprise SaaS vendors are aggressively “agent-ifying” their existing platforms faster than IT departments can react, evaluate, or disable them.

To help organizations navigate this shift, this report explores these urgent challenges using recent industry survey data and introduces an identity-centric framework designed to solve them by providing:

- Discovery and unified visibility to see agents and help satisfy audit requirements.
- Lifecycle management and clear ownership to drive accountability for agent behavior from creation through retirement.
- Real-time governance and audit to control agent access and help prove compliance.
- Proactive protection and response to stop threats before they escalate into reportable incidents.

Without this foundation in place, the rush to innovate is currently creating four core problems for enterprises:
- A visibility gap due to unmanaged shadow AI and a lack of a unified agent inventory, making it difficult to demonstrate comprehensive control to auditors.
- A critical governance deficit, as agents are granted excessive access without clear ownership or adherence to the principle of least privilege, which may violate core tenets of SOC 2 and GDPR.
- A SecOps vulnerability, characterized by the inability to proactively protect against and respond to AI agent threats that operate at machine speed, rendering manual oversight obsolete.
- A compliance breakdown, where traditional, point-in-time compliance checks fail to keep pace with the dynamic, autonomous actions of these new machine identities.

Furthermore, global regulatory frameworks are rapidly evolving to ensure these technologies are deployed safely. As noted by Gartner, with AI regulation projected to cover 50% of the world’s economies by 2027, organizations need to be prepared. The reality is that organizations lacking agile, automated, and identity-centric governance programs already face significant compliance risks, financial penalties, and stalled innovation today. Because regulations apply equally whether an actor is human or machine, the rise of AI agents simply represents a massive expansion of the non-human identities (NHIs) that must now be brought under these mandates.

By anchoring AI security in identity, organizations can build the agile, compliant, and secure foundation required to innovate with confidence in the agentic era.

## The Agentic Era Is Here: A New Workforce, A New Identity Crisis

Generative AI was just the prologue; the real disruption lies in autonomous action. We have crossed the threshold from AI that merely converses to AI that executes—systems that independently log in, authenticate, manipulate data, and navigate critical enterprise infrastructure without human intervention. This shift defines the “agentic era,” a fundamental operational pivot that promises massive productivity gains. However, realizing the vision of a fully functioning “agentic workforce”—where complex multi-agent systems collaborate and execute enterprise-wide operations—requires proving these systems are secure through rigorous behavior testing, lifecycle management, and continuous governance cycles.

Currently, the race to deploy these agents is breaking traditional, human-centric security and compliance models. To understand why, consider the mechanics of standard identity tools: traditional Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) relies on human interaction, like a biometric scan or an SMS code, which a background autonomous agent cannot fulfill. Similarly, point-in-time access reviews, which typically happen quarterly, are practically useless against machine identities that can autonomously spin up, request elevated privileges, execute a task, and spin down in a matter of seconds. When an agent goes rogue or makes an error at machine speed, legacy systems may lack the framework to immediately assign human accountability or revoke access.

Despite these critical security risks, the business drivers pushing for AI agent adoption are incredibly compelling. Organizations are highly motivated by the automation of repetitive tasks (cited by 85% of respondents as a top-3 business outcome), faster or better decision-making (67%), and cost reduction (62%).

**What is driving the rush to AI agents?**
- Automation of repetitive tasks: **85%**
- Faster or better decision-making: **67%**
- Cost reduction: **62%**  
*(Percentage of respondents who cited this as a top-3 business outcome)*

Fueled by these business drivers, adoption is occurring at an unprecedented scale. Ninety-one percent of respondents are either already using AI agents in production or actively evaluating them, with 47% having agents operate in production environments. Organizations draw from a mix of channels to source these agents, including packaged assistants in enterprise software (69%), in-house development (56%), and third-party vendors (54%). As these non-human identities quickly outpace the number of human employees, the rush to deploy them creates a fundamental conflict between rapid innovation and enterprise security.

## The Urgent Problem: Why AI Agents Create a Massive Security Blind Spot

AI agents introduce a new class of identity risk that most organizations are not prepared for, creating pervasive security concerns.

**Top 3 concerns:**
- **69%** - Data or privacy breach
- **55%** - Unauthorized access
- **47%** - Compliance or regulatory violations

Data security and privacy is a top concern, with 69% of respondents ranking a data breach as a top-3 security priority. The issue is closely followed by unauthorized access to sensitive systems (55%) and, critically, compliance or regulatory violations (47%).

These are not abstract fears. According to survey takers, the majority of AI agents (**73%**) access sensitive customer data. Nearly half also access HR data (48%) and financial records (43%). With only 3% of organizations reporting that their agents access no sensitive data, the attack surface has expanded dramatically.

> **97%** of all AI agents have access to sensitive data

This risk is magnified by a profound lack of visibility and control. Only **30%** of organizations have complete, real-time visibility into their agentic workforce, leaving the rest with dangerous blind spots. “Shadow AI” is a major part of this problem, as 44% of respondents lack formal processes for detecting unsanctioned AI tools.

![Visual diagram mapping hidden risks: Shadow AI, Visible AI usage (only 30%), Unclear ownership, Excessive privileges, and Compliance blind spots]

Beyond data exfiltration and compliance fines, the actual operationalization of these agents is frequently overlooked, leading to devastating system impacts. When non-human identities are deployed without strict lifecycle controls, clear ownership, or behavioral guardrails, their ability to execute tasks at machine speed can quickly overwhelm APIs, exhaust resources, and trigger critical system failures.

Compounding this visibility gap, access controls are woefully inadequate. While most organizations are attempting to enforce the principle of least privilege, our research shows the vast majority are failing to do so completely. A staggering 69% of organizations have only partially implemented least-privilege access for their AI agents, leaving them dangerously exposed. The result is a startlingly small number of organizations that have gotten it right: a mere **11%** have fully implemented least-privilege access for AI agents.

This lack of foundational controls, combined with the enterprise rush to adopt autonomous technologies, has created a massive Deployment-Confidence Gap. As noted earlier, while an overwhelming **91%** of organizations are actively deploying or evaluating AI agents, this rapid adoption is vastly outpacing their ability to secure it. When asked to rate their “ability to manage AI agent security risks,” only a small fraction—just **21%**—report being “very” or “extremely” confident. The vast majority (79%) are moving forward with only moderate, low, or zero confidence in their risk management capabilities.

This deficit extends beyond visibility into a deeper, structural governance crisis. The very frameworks for managing these new digital workers are either incomplete or fundamentally broken. For instance, our data shows that the concept of ownership—the cornerstone of accountability—is dangerously diffused. A staggering 65% of organizations assign AI agent ownership to entire teams rather than a specific individual. While collaborative ownership structures can work, they require clearly defined roles and responsibilities; without them, proving compliant oversight to auditors becomes incredibly difficult and forensic investigations can stall when an agent performs an unauthorized action. Another 17% have no clear ownership structure at all, leaving high-privilege agents to operate as completely orphaned identities. Every AI agent, regardless of whether it is managed by a person or a team, requires a structured and well-defined ownership model tied to its lifecycle to ensure continuous accountability.

When drilling down specifically into AI agents over the past 12 months, 18% of organizations have experienced direct adverse outcomes, including the sharing of sensitive information (44%), unauthorized system access (39%), and the exposure of credentials (39%). With governance policies still in development at 57% of organizations and ownership structures often unclear, the foundation for accountable, auditable AI usage is alarmingly weak.

This is not just a security failure; it is a business inhibitor. The inability to ensure and prove compliance for AI agents stalls innovation, increases the risk of regulatory fines, and erodes customer trust. Without a new framework for identity, the promise of the agentic era will remain unrealized, crippled by the risks it creates.
