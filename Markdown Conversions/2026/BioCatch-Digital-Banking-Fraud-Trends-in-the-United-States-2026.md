Trends
More than three-fourths (78%) of U.S.-based participants in BioCatch’s global industry survey, The future
of digital trust, report increasing fraud attempts at their organization, while 64% say fraud losses at their
institution are also increasing.
Job scam attempts increased by 44% among BioCatch’s U.S. customers in the last year. Typically, job
scam victims receive and respond to unsolicited communications offering simple online work via social
media or instant messaging applications. After pretending to hire their victims for a legitimate opportunity,
fraudsters will request personal details including social security numbers and photo IDs, which, if shared,
can result in identity theft.
Impersonation scam losses in the U.S. more than doubled between 2025 and 2026, with fraudsters
commonly posing as representatives of legitimate organizations and conning victims into sending them
money under a range of false pretenses.
BioCatch’s U.S. customers also reported a 50% increase in phishing attempts in the past year. Phishing
often provides the foundation for credential theft, with harvesting links distributed via social media,
messaging apps, and email — increasingly at great scale, with AI integration enabling more personalized
materials aimed at potential victims.

Although they represent a smaller share of overall fraud attempts, investment scams
have emerged as the costliest fraud type in the U.S. Fraudsters promise high returns
on cryptocurrency, forex, and commodities investments, using spoofed websites and
fraudulent broker platforms to create a false sense of legitimacy.
Purchase scams:
$28 million
in reported attempted losses
Online marketplaces have become a hub for counterfeit listings and brand
impersonations, with AI-generated content facilitating increasingly convincing fake
storefronts and deepfakes. Fraudsters typically advertise high-demand goods at prices
well below the market average and employ urgency tactics such as flash sales and low-
stock alerts to force impulse purchases from unsuspecting buyers.

Government and law enforcement scams typically transpire via email and/or phone calls,
with fraudsters using spoofing technology to appear as representatives of legitimate
agencies. To increase perceived legitimacy, fraudsters provide fictitious badge numbers
and use official agency letterheads. They threaten to arrest account holders if those

methods such as gift cards.

BioCatch geolocation data shows where fraudulent sessions originate — whether those sessions are
conducted by a fraudster or a genuine user under the direction of a scammer. Analysis of fraud sessions
reported by BioCatch customers in the U.S. between June 2025 and May 2026 shows 83.9% of fraud
attempts (worth $1.4 billion in would-be losses) originated from devices located in-country — likely due to
the continued increase in social engineering scams, where scammers (located anywhere in the world)
manipulate victims into legitimately authorizing scam transactions.
The remaining 16.1% of fraud sessions, representing $263 million in attempted losses, originated across
186 countries. International activity was concentrated in several regions. Latin America accounted for a
significant share, consistent with the region’s well-documented fraud networks and cryptocurrency cash-out
infrastructure that criminal organizations often use to move illicit funds. Criminal organizations operate
directly from LATAM to exploit local resources, conducting localized fraud operations. Because they operate
from physical locations within the region rather than using VPNs or proxies to mask their activity, the
geolocation data reflects actual operator locations.

Case overview
This case study examines a multi-stage account takeover against a customer of a U.S. financial institution.
The attack involves the deployment of a remote access trojan (RAT). The attacker gained recurrent access
to a victim’s device, allowing this fraudster to make unauthorized transactions across multiple geographic
locations.
The remote access obtained via computer subsequently enabled a new mobile device to be registered on
the customer’s profile and a new account to be opened.
The multiple fraud-flagged sessions highlight BioCatch’s ability to identify suspicious behavioral patterns
across individual sessions, enabling real-time intervention to prevent attackers from extracting significant
value from compromised accounts.
Social engineering in remote access
RAT-based ATO attacks represent a fast-growing fraud vector. With complete control over a victim’s
device, fraudsters can bypass traditional multi-factor authentication (MFA), security questions and
behavioral biometrics. BioCatch’s threat indicators specifically identified the RAT presence, demonstrating
the critical need for device-level fraud detection.
Social Engineering represents a critical component of remote access cases. Rather than targeting
technological vulnerabilities, fraudsters exploit human emotions and trust to get their victims to divulge
confidential information and gain access to their device.
A common example is fraudsters pretending to be from technology companies such as Microsoft, Apple or
Amazon and claiming they need to fix an urgent issue.
Fraud controls rely heavily on trusted device signals, however remote access can exploit this by hijacking
the victim’s own genuine device and turning a legitimate session into a potential attack vector.
Once remote access tools have been installed on a victims’ device, the fraudster has full control over
trusted endpoints, bypassing multi-factor authentication (MFA) protections. Without remote access,
fraudsters would only have stolen credentials, which would be far less effective if MFA is enabled.

Conclusion
This case study shows how behavioral intelligence
identified high-risk signals from the very early stages of this
account takeover. RAT malware, proxy abuse, device
switching, and geolocation anomalies were all detected,
giving the bank several points at which to intervene and
stop this attack before the account holder lost any money.
This highlights the importance of integrating real-time
decision-making into fraud controls. BioCatch’s detection

scored it at 1000 (the maximum risk score), meaning this

collecting more than 3,000 anonymized data points — keystroke and mouse activity, touch screen
