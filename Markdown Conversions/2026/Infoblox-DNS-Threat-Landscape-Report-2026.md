Between June 2025 and June 2026, Infoblox monitored the evolving cyber threat
landscape through different lenses. Our continuously expanding visibility revealed
new findings that highlight the rapid industrialization of cybercrime. Key figures
below demonstrate how adversaries increasingly rely on automation, highly
scalable infrastructure, and specialized criminal services to execute campaigns
with unprecedented efficiency and reach.
- Infoblox’s DNS visibility expanded by more than 24% year over year. During the
reporting period, Infoblox observed over 120 million newly registered domains,
of which more than 22% were weaponized or exhibited threat characteristics.
- Traffic distribution systems (TDS) affected the largest share of customers
(96%), reflecting their critical role in cybercriminal campaigns by routing
victims through traffic brokers, profiling tools, and redirectors before
delivering tailored lures.
- 65% of Infoblox Threat Defense Cloud customers queried domains
associated with residential proxy networks, highlighting the widespread
use of infrastructures for rent that enables malicious activity to blend in with
legitimate internet traffic.
- 88% of threat-related domains were observed in a maximum of one
environment, while 44% were active for just a single day, illustrating both
the scale of domain weaponization and the rapid infrastructure rotation.
- The number of named threat actors increased 67% year over year, while
unnamed threat clusters grew 97%, reflecting both increased adversary
activity and improved visibility into emerging campaigns.
- 5.3 billion+ unique leaked credentials and 373,000 brand abuse
incidents were identified, highlighting the scale of digital risk exposure.

Credential and Data Theft at Scale
An Android banking trojan malware-as-a-service (MaaS) platform provides
cybercriminals with the tools and infrastructure needed to steal banking
credentials and personal data at scale. The malware captures banking
credentials, SMS one-time passwords (OTPs), call content, biometrics,
contacts, and other sensitive information, enabling account takeover,
identity theft, and long-term fraud.
The MaaS operator rents or sells malware, command-and-control (C2)
panels, Android packaging tools, and supporting infrastructure to multiple
scam groups, turning credential theft into an on-demand service.
The platform’s C2 infrastructure relies primarily on .top domains (39 of 42
active C2 domains), with a smaller number using .xyz, .vip, and .pro. These
domains are consistently registered through Domain International Services
and Namemart, and use DomainNameDNS name servers.
Attacks begin by luring victims to fake websites that impersonate trusted
banks or government services. From there, a customizable malware kit
delivers one of several Android banking trojan variants, allowing operators
to tailor campaigns to different targets.

Short-Lived Campaigns
While phishing infrastructure is often large and resilient, the phishing
pages themselves are increasingly short-lived. Attackers frequently rotate
domains, hosting providers, and URLs, allowing campaigns to remain
active while evading blocklists and traditional detection methods.
A good example is a phishing campaign uncovered by Infoblox Threat Intel
in December 2025 that targeted student single sign-on (SSO) portals at
more than 18 U.S. universities. Using the Evilginx adversary-in-the-middle
framework, attackers created convincing phishing pages designed to steal
credentials and bypass multi-factor authentication.
Each phishing URL closely resembled a legitimate SSO portal but
included a unique random path and typically expired within 24 hours.
This short lifespan reduced the detection window, made investigation
more difficult, and helped attackers stay ahead of security controls.
By analyzing DNS patterns associated with the campaign, Infoblox
identified nearly 70 malicious domains and exposed the hidden
infrastructure supporting the attacks.
