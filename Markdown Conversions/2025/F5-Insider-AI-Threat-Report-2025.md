## Introduction

> “These numbers should be a wake-up call. We’re seeing executives racing to implement AI without fully understanding the risks, frontline employees using it unsupervised, and even trusted security professionals breaking their own rules. We know inappropriate use of AI can be catastrophic for enterprises, and this isn’t a future threat—it’s already happening inside organizations today.”
> 
> **Donnchadh Casey**  
> VP of Sales, F5

AI has become an important component of daily work. But as employees lean on AI tools to brainstorm, draft, and entertain them, their use of these tools largely goes unmonitored.

Such usage sits outside guardrails. This report examines how workers across roles and industries are using—and misusing—AI inside their organizations.

A clear pattern is emerging: AI usage is accelerating faster than internal policy, and the biggest risks now come from inside the building.

C-suite leaders, mid-level decision-makers, and entry-level employees all show varying degrees of confusion, misplaced confidence, or indifference toward secure AI use. In highly regulated industries such as banking and healthcare, these behaviors expose organizations to reputational harm, compliance breaches, and operational disruption.

The data is unequivocal: AI fluency and AI responsibility are not keeping pace with AI adoption—and enterprises must urgently confront this gap.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** FULL-TIME OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **45%** trust AI more than their co-workers
- **38%** would rather have AI as a manager than a human
- **34%** would quit their job if their employer didn't allow the use of AI

## AI is Creating a Trust Transition

AI isn’t just another workplace tool — it’s becoming an entity employees lean on emotionally and operationally. For many, AI already feels more reliable, more impartial, and, at times, more capable than their colleagues or managers.

This shift has created a trust transition where employees willingly transferring trust — and sometimes authority — from humans to AI systems. This transference of trust to AI can expedite adoption but it also blinds employees to the risks.

When nearly half of employees say they trust AI more than their co-workers, or that they would quit if they couldn’t use AI at work, it signals a new power dynamic inside the enterprise. AI is influencing retention, morale, productivity, and decision-making — but without the governance structures that normally accompany that level of influence.

The stakes are even higher in regulated industries, where employees use on AI without fully knowing the regulatory compliance requirements and legal ramifications.

## Internal Misuse of AI

The most significant AI threat to enterprises today isn’t external attackers. It’s employees who use AI without understanding the security implications.

Workers increasingly view AI as a shortcut to getting things done—a way to “outsource” tedious tasks, move faster, or appear more productive. But with that convenience comes casual risk-taking in the form of using proprietary information in GenAI prompts, accessing sensitive data with AI assistance, or ignoring usage policies altogether.

More than half of employees admit they would use AI against policy if it made their job easier. A third have already used it to access sensitive documents. And nearly everyone believes leadership or IT would detect an AI-led breach — a level of misplaced confidence that could accelerate the next major internal incident.

This isn’t malicious behavior. It’s misaligned incentives and misplaced trust—and it’s becoming one of the biggest open doors in enterprise security.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** FULL-TIME OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **84%** are somewhat or very confident their company’s CEO/IT team would catch an AI-led breach
- **52%** of employees are willing to use AI to make their job easier, even if it means violating policy
- **28%** of employees have already used AI to access sensitive data or documents

## The C-Suite is Leading AI, but Doesn’t Understand It

Executives are leading AI adoption from the top—but many lack the fluency needed to govern it. C-level leaders use AI frequently, often without understanding what it’s doing under the hood or whether they’re using it responsibly.

Their confidence is disproportionately high with most believing IT would detect an AI-driven breach instantly, and many trusting AI over human colleagues. Yet a notable portion cannot explain the difference between an AI agent and a virtual employee—despite overseeing multimillion-dollar systems that may depend on both.

This creates a critical governance gap. When strategic decision-makers misunderstand the tools they champion, enterprises risk building policy, investment, and operations based on ill-informed practices.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** C-LEVEL OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **67%** are willing to use AI if it makes their job easier, even if it goes against policy
- **66%** are very confident an AI-led breach would be detected by IT
- **58%** say they trust AI more than they trust their co-workers

## Entry-Level Employee Usage Risks

Entry-level workers often the least equipped to evaluate the risks of AI usage. Many feel disconnected from usage policies, confused about what’s allowed, or indifferent altogether.

For them, AI becomes:
- A productivity crutch
- A learning substitute
- A fast way to complete tasks they don’t fully understand

The combination of inexperience, high reliance and trust in AI, and low policy awareness creates a uniquely vulnerable risk profile. And with younger workers expecting AI access as a workplace norm, companies face an emerging generation of employees who see secure AI use as optional.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** ENTRY-LEVEL OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **37%** wouldn’t feel guilty if they broke AI protocol
- **33%** say they don’t know what an AI agent is
- **30%** say they don’t care about their company’s AI policy
- **25%** would quit their job if they couldn’t use AI on AI
- **21%** say their company’s policies on AI are unclear, so they use AI as they wish
- **16%** can’t tell the difference between an AI agent and a virtual employee

## Financial Services and Banking

One of the most compliance-heavy industry is quietly bending—and sometimes breaking—its own rules in the age of AI. Employees trust AI enough to use it even when prohibited, and many find usage policies too restrictive or outdated to reflect the realities of modern workflows.

AI is becoming a shadow decision-maker in a space that has little room for error. Submitting proprietary financial data for analysis, drafting regulated communications with AI, or using AI tools without approval can have direct compliance and audit implications.

When over half of employees are knowingly bypassing policies, unmonitored AI usage becomes compliance risk embedded in everyday work.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** FINANCE + BANKING OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **60%** of employees in financial and banking say they use AI tools even if it violates company policy
- **43%** say they trust AI more than their co-workers
- **36%** say they don’t feel guilty about it

## Healthcare

In healthcare, the people closest to sensitive patient data are increasingly treating AI as an ally often ignoring the risks. Many trust AI more than colleagues and use it to accelerate administrative tasks, documentation, and research—often without confirming whether the tool is approved or secure.

The issue isn’t intent; it’s awareness. Employees may assume an AI tool is harmless if it helps them work faster. But when patient data intersects with generative models, even small missteps can escalate into privacy breaches, regulatory violations, and patient mistrust.

Healthcare’s challenge isn’t resistance to AI — it’s unintentional overconfidence in it.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** HEALTHCARE OFFICE WORKERS IN THE US

- **55%** of healthcare employees say they know and follow their organization’s AI policy (the lowest of any regulated industry)
- **31%** of healthcare employees trust AI more than their co-workers
- **27%** would rather report to AI than a human supervisor
- **21%** say their company’s policies on AI are unclear, so they use AI as they wish

## Security

AI isn’t only a risk for the untrained. Even the people responsible for defending against AI threats aren’t immune to risky behavior. AI trust among security professionals is high, and many admit they would break policy if AI made their job easier.

This reveals a critical organizational tension as employees tasked with modeling risk mitigation are simultaneously participating in risky AI usage.

Security teams also report unclear policies and uncertainty about IT’s ability to catch AI-related leaks. When defenders are this uncertain or unwilling to subscribe to AI usage policies, the organization’s overall security posture becomes unbalanced.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** SECURITY WORKERS IN THE US

- **58%** of security employees say they trust AI more than their own colleagues
- **48%** say their company’s AI policy is unclear, so they use it as they wish
- **42%** say they’d use AI and break policy if it made their work easier
- **31%** say they’d look for another role if their company’s AI made an error

## IT and Telecoms

IT professionals are some of the most enthusiastic about using AI at work, and the most confident that they’re on top of its threats.

No industry embraces AI more enthusiastically than IT and telecom—and no industry is more confident that it can manage the risks. But this confidence can mask important blind spots.

IT workers use AI frequently, trust it deeply, and believe they can detect misuse or distinguish AI agents from virtual employees. Yet high usage combined with high trust can normalize risky behaviors such as using AI to handle sensitive data, pulling proprietary information into prompts, or automating tasks without verifying security guardrails.

Here, the challenge isn’t adoption — it’s ensuring that confidence doesn’t drift into complacency.

**DATA AUDIENCE:** IT + TELECOM WORKERS IN THE US

- **92%** say that if AI was used to access or leak sensitive data, the IT team or the company’s CEO would know
- **78%** believe they can tell the difference between an AI agent and a virtual employee
- **55%** would prefer to have AI as a manager than a human
- **52%** would quit their job if their employer didn’t allow the use of AI

## Methodology and Conclusion

### Methodology
The total sample size was 1,002 full-time office workers in the United States, aged 25–65. The survey was conducted from June 11–17, 2025. Censuswide abides by and employs members of the Market Research Society which is based on the ESOMAR principles.

### Conclusion
Companies need to expand their definition of AI security to go beyond systems and technology to cover people, behaviors, and the breakdown of trust.

AI is redefining workplace behaviors, expectations, and risks faster than organizations can adapt. Employees trust AI, rely on it, and increasingly treat it as a core partner in their daily workflows.

To mitigate internal and external AI risks, enterprises need more than technical controls. They need:
- Clear policy grounded in real employee behavior
- Industry-specific risk modeling
- Guardrails that balance innovation with security
- Tools that secure AI wherever it runs — SaaS, cloud, edge, or self-hosted

AI is here. The question is whether enterprises will secure it as quickly as employees adopt it.
