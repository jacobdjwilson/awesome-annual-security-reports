Healthcare organizations continue to be a unique challenge for information security professionals. Unlike many other industries where payment cards or intellectual property are the primary targets, healthcare is dominated by the compromise of medical and personal records.

### The Insider Threat
Employees in healthcare—doctors, nurses, administrative staff—have legitimate access to sensitive patient records. Unfortunately, this access is sometimes abused. Whether it’s looking up the medical records of a celebrity, snooping on a neighbor, or stealing patient data to commit medical identity theft, internal actors account for nearly half of all breaches in this industry.

### Ransomware's Grip on Hospitals
While insiders cause many breaches, external actors are far from idle. Ransomware has found a very comfortable home in the healthcare sector. Hospitals and clinics operate under immense pressure where availability of systems is a matter of life and death. Threat actors understand this urgency, making healthcare organizations prime targets for extortion via ransomware.

Things to consider:
- **Need-to-Know Access**: Limit employee access to patient records strictly to what is required for their specific role.
- **Audit Trails**: Regularly review access logs to spot unauthorized snooping early.
- **Employee Training**: Train staff on the risks of phishing and social engineering, which often serve as the initial entry point for ransomware.

Retailers continue to be a primary target for financially motivated cybercriminals looking for payment card data. While point-of-sale (POS) intrusions remain a dominant threat, web application attacks targeting e-commerce platforms have also grown in frequency.

### The POS Battleground
Attackers routinely target POS terminals using RAM scrapers and malware installed via compromised third-party vendor credentials or weak remote access controls. 

Things to consider:
- **Vendor Risk Management**: Strictly monitor and restrict third-party vendor access to your internal network and POS systems.
- **Monitor Outbound Traffic**: Watch for anomalous outbound traffic that could indicate command-and-control (C2) communication or data exfiltration.

Attack the Humans!
Phishing is the most common social attack vector. Across datasets, roughly 7% to 14% of users tested will click on malicious links or attachments in phishing simulations.

The human element remains one of the most persistent vulnerabilities in information security. Despite decades of awareness training, attackers continue to successfully manipulate users through phishing, pretexting, and social engineering. 

### The Anatomy of Phishing
Phishing campaigns range from broad, untargeted spam to highly sophisticated spear-phishing emails tailored to specific individuals. Attackers use these methods primarily to deliver malware or harvest credentials. 

---

Ransomware is the Most Profitable Form of Writing
Ransomware has exploded in popularity among cybercriminals. By encrypting victim files and demanding payment (typically in cryptocurrency) for the decryption key, attackers have found a highly lucrative and straightforward business model.

- **Low Barrier to Entry**: Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS) allows even low-skill criminals to launch devastating attacks.
- **Immediate Impact**: Organizations face crippling downtime, forcing many to pay the ransom to restore operations.

Data subsets
We already mentioned the subset of incidents that passed
our quality requirements, but as part of our analysis there
are other instances where we define subsets of data. These
subsets consist of legitimate incidents that would eclipse
smaller trends if left in. These are removed and analyzed
separately (as called out in the relevant sections). This
year, the only subset analyzed separately across the board
consisted of web servers that were identified as secondary
targets (such as taking over a website to spread malware).
Finally, we create some subsets to help further our analysis.
This year we created a botnet subset that helps us analyze
the impact of botnets on the data. As with last year, we left
this subset in the data for the core analysis and removed
it in some figures to allow the other results to present
themselves. Anytime we did this it is noted in the figure
header and/or supporting text.
Non-incident data
The 2016 DBIR includes sections that required the analysis
of data that did not fit into our usual categories of “incident”
or “breach.” Examples of non-incident data include malware,
patching, phishing, DDoS and other types of data. The
sample sizes for non-incident data tend to be much larger
than the incident data, but from fewer sources. We make
every effort to normalize the data. For example reporting
on the median organization rather than the average of all
data. We also attempt to combine multiple contributors with
similar data to conduct the analysis wherever possible. Once
analysis is complete, we try to discuss our findings with the
relevant contributor(s) to validate it against their knowledge
of the data.
